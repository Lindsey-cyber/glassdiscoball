1

A ring in the brain

椭球体

In the middle of the fly brain is a doughnut-shaped structure, the ellipsoid body. About 46 E-PG neurons tile it in 16 wedges. At any moment a single bump of activity sits somewhere on the ring, and when the fly turns, the bump turns with it, even in complete darkness. The fly is integrating its own angular velocity, like a sailor doing dead reckoning.

That is what the ring on the left shows. Each wedge's orange is the mean firing of the E-PG neurons in it. The strip underneath is the protocerebral bridge, where the same neurons hand the bump to P-EN cells, which shift it left or right as the fly turns.

Try it. Switch Auto-turn off and drag the fly a quarter turn. The error should stay small. Leave it in darkness for a minute and the decoded heading slowly drifts, as a real fly's does; turn the landmark on and it snaps back.
2

Wired from the connectome

连接组

Nobody hand-designed this network. The 152 neurons and their 129,473 synapses come from the hemibrain, an electron-microscopy reconstruction of a female fly's brain. Each neuron's input is the sum of its partners' activity weighted by how many synapses connect them.

Only 49 numbers were fitted, never the synapse counts: one gain for each pair of cell types, plus a bias and a time constant per type. With raw synapse counts and a single overall gain, no stable bump forms.

Connection matrix 152 × 152 · hemibrain

Rows receive, columns send, sorted by cell type and bridge glomerulus. Red excites, blue inhibits. The diagonal stripes are the ring: each wedge talks most to its neighbours. The matrix follows the circuit you pick on the left.

3

The shape of activity

状态空间

At each moment, the 46 E-PG firing rates are one point in a 46-dimensional space. As the fly turns, that point moves. If the circuit really is a compass, the point can only go round: the cloud of all moments should form a closed loop, one point on the loop per heading.

A loop in a picture is suggestive, but a projection can create or hide loops. Topology lets us ask the question in all 46 dimensions at once.

4

Birth and death of a loop

生与死

Pick a distance ε. Join every two recorded moments that are closer than ε, and fill in every triangle whose three sides are all joined. Now grow ε from zero. At first there are only scattered points. Then neighbours join into one ring, and a hole appears: a loop is born. Grow ε further and edges start to cross the ring, the triangles fill the hole, and the loop dies.

Persistent homology records every loop's birth and death. Plot each loop as a point at (birth, death) and you get the persistence diagram found in the papers. Noise makes loops that die almost as soon as they are born, close to the diagonal. A real ring makes one point far above it.

How do we know a loop is not chance? The shuffle test slides each neuron's time series by a random offset. Every neuron keeps its own firing statistics, but the coordination between neurons is destroyed. The longest loop any shuffle produces sets the grey band. A loop that rises above the band is real.

5

Break the circuit

破坏

If the ring in the activity comes from the wiring, then breaking the wiring should break the ring. Each experiment below runs a fresh circuit for 60 s of a fly wandering in the dark, then computes the persistence diagram and a 20-shuffle test, exactly as above. All four diagrams share one scale.

about 15 s on a laptop
Why Δ7 matters. Δ7 neurons spread inhibition across the whole bridge. Without them, excitation runs away and every wedge saturates: the state collapses to a single point and the loop has nothing to go round. With the wiring shuffled within each pair of cell types, the cell-type totals are kept but the neighbour structure is lost, and so is the ring.

What this shows, and what it doesn't

The anatomy and the activity have the same shape. A ring of neurons, wired as the connectome says with only cell-type-level tuning, produces activity whose 46-dimensional state space is a single persistent loop. Breaking the wiring removes the loop.

This is a simulation constrained by the connectome, not a recording from a fly. Neurotransmitter signs (E-PG, P-EN, P-EG excitatory; Δ7 inhibitory) are standard assumptions. A long H1 bar shows that the states form a loop; it does not by itself prove a ring attractor, which also needs the bump to persist and move continuously. Real recordings of mouse head-direction cells show the same topology (Chaudhuri et al. 2019).
