The fly's compass is a ring. Is its activity one too?

A fruit fly keeps track of where it is facing with a ring of neurons in its brain. Here that circuit runs live, wired with real synapse counts from the fly connectome. Turn the fly, watch the bump of activity follow, then ask topology whether the network's activity lies on a ring.

What you are looking at

The anatomy is a ring. About 46 E-PG neurons tile a doughnut-shaped brain region, the ellipsoid body, in 16 wedges. A single bump of activity sits on the ring and moves as the fly turns, even in darkness.

The wiring. 152 neurons and their synapse counts come from the hemibrain connectome. P-EN neurons connect one wedge over, so when the fly turns they push the bump around; Δ7 neurons spread inhibition across the circuit.

What was fitted. The synapse counts are never changed. Only 49 numbers were fitted, one per pair of cell types plus a bias and a time constant per type, so the bump tracks heading in the dark. With raw counts and a single global gain, no stable bump forms.

Is the activity a ring? One long H1 bar well above the shuffle line means the 46-dimensional activity traces out a loop: the network's state space has the same shape as its anatomy. Remove Δ7 or shuffle the wiring and the loop disappears.

This is a simulation constrained by the connectome, not a recording from a fly. Neurotransmitter signs (E-PG, P-EN, P-EG excitatory; Δ7 inhibitory) are standard assumptions. A long H1 bar shows a loop; it does not by itself prove a ring attractor.
